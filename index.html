STEM education in Australia · 2013–2025

Who will build Australia's future?

Science, technology, engineering and maths (STEM) skills power everything from hospitals to power grids. This page follows Australian students from the school classroom to university and into STEM jobs, and asks who joins, who stays, and who drops out along the way.

By Lim Hui Zern · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 · October 2026

To fill future STEM jobs, Australia needs a steady flow of young people who study these subjects. For readers in Malaysia this story is close to home: more than 23,000 students from Malaysia are enrolled with Australian universities, and many of them study STEM.

We look at four stages of the journey: how well students learn maths at school, what they choose to study at university, where they come from, and how many women stay in STEM until they get a job.

What counts as STEM here? For university data we follow the Australian Government's STEM Equity Monitor: natural & physical sciences (which includes maths), information technology, engineering and agriculture & environmental studies. Health courses such as nursing and medicine are counted separately.

01

It starts with maths at school

Every few years the OECD's PISA test checks how well 15-year-olds can use maths in real-life problems. A strong maths foundation is the first door into STEM. Australia sits in the upper-middle of the world, ahead of the OECD average but well behind East Asian leaders like Singapore and Japan.

02

At university, IT is the breakout subject

Australian universities had 1.68 million students in 2024. Business (management & commerce) is still the most popular field, but its numbers have stopped growing. Information technology has grown faster than any other field and has jumped over education, sciences and engineering.

03

Where in Australia is STEM studied?

About one in four university enrolments is in a STEM field, but this varies across the country. STEM study is concentrated in the big east-coast cities, especially Melbourne and Sydney, where the largest universities are.

04

A global classroom

More than a third of Australia's university students (589,000 in 2024) come from overseas. They fill many STEM classrooms. In IT, international students now graduate in greater numbers than Australians.

05

The leaky pipeline: where do the women go?

Girls take almost half of all Year 12 STEM subjects, but at every later stage their share shrinks. Experts call this the "leaky pipeline": women leak out of STEM between school, university and work.

→

What does this mean for Australia's future STEM workforce?

Australia's STEM pipeline is growing, but it depends on a narrow base: one fast-growing field, a few big-city universities, international students, and mostly men.

1. IT is booming

IT enrolments have nearly tripled since 2015, while sciences, engineering and agriculture have grown slowly.

2. Reliant on the world

International students, including thousands from Malaysia, now produce most of Australia's new IT graduates.

3. Women are still missing

Women make up 47% of Year 12 STEM enrolments but only 16% of the STEM workforce. Closing this gap is the biggest untapped source of STEM talent.
